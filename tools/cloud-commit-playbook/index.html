Cloud Commit & Usage Playbook

A playbook is not a document — it is the order decisions get made in. This one runs the way an enterprise cloud deal desk runs: governance first (a discount matrix that says who may approve what), the renewal gate second (no discount clears if it shrinks the renewal), and the value levers third (the commit, sized against real usage, with the waste showing). Three tabs, all of it live, all of it sample data.

Provenance & Operating Record: Built from 15+ years in commercial contracts, deal desk, and revenue operations across contracts up to $300M and 3,000+ SKUs under management. This workbook is an original sample built in November 2026 for this site — its structure follows enterprise cloud deal-desk governance and its commit mechanics reflect public cloud committed-use documentation (Google Cloud CUD documentation and FinOps Foundation material). Every threshold, approver name, and dollar figure is generic fictional sample data.

How to run this playbook

Shared live matrix · 25 workbook formulas

Start on the Discount Matrix: the thresholds and approver names are inputs, so re-tier it and every route in the workbook moves; set a discount in the Route-a-discount block and the matrix names its approver. The Renewal Check takes the current TCV, the renewal's list value, and the discount being asked: it returns the renewal TCV, the verdict under the ratchet rule, and the deepest discount that would still grow the renewal — with the approver for that counter-play read back through the matrix. The Commit & Usage section sizes a spend-based commitment against expected usage: what the commitment covers, what bills at full price, what you pay, what you save, the effective discount, and the commitment dollars paid and never used.

Where this playbook comes from

        
Build provenance, design choices & operating boundaries

      


      

        

          
Original sample & deliberate choices

          

            This workbook is an original sample, built in November 2026 for this site. Its structure follows how enterprise cloud deal desks actually organize their playbooks — the pattern that falls out of the real thing: the approval rules sit in front of the plays, not inside them; the standard path is short and the exceptions carry the weight; and every play ends in a routing line, a named owner for the next step, rather than an explanation. The commit mechanics are the public ones — committed-use discounts are documented openly by the cloud providers (Google Cloud's published CUD documentation is the reference here) and by practitioner bodies like the FinOps Foundation. No internal system, codename, real threshold, figure or SKU from any company is carried over; every number on this page is a generic sample value.
          

          

            Three choices in the build are deliberate. First, the matrix is the input: on most sites a discount matrix is a screenshot, and a screenshot cannot be argued with — here a visitor can re-tier it and watch the routes change, which is what a matrix is for. Second, the ratchet computes its own counter-play: a blocked renewal does not just say no, it prices the deepest discount that still grows the TCV and names who would approve it, which turns a refusal into a negotiation position. Third, the waste line: commitment tools usually show coverage and savings and stop; this one also shows the commitment dollars paid and never used, because that is the number that decides whether the commit was sized right.
          

        


        

          
Inputs, logic, limits

          

            Inputs: the matrix's band thresholds and approver names, the discount asked, the current TCV and renewal list value, and the commit block (expected usage at list, the monthly commitment, the commit discount, the term in months). Logic: the band chain and the nested-IF routing are the workbook's own formulas; the ratchet verdict compares the discounted renewal TCV against the current TCV, and the counter-play discount is the exact depth at which the two are equal, capped at zero; the commit tab covers usage up to the list value the commitment buys at its discount, bills the rest at full price, and counts any commitment left unused as paid waste. Limits, stated plainly: the thresholds and approvers are generic sample values — every real company's matrix is its own; the commit mechanics are a simplified sample of spend-based committed-use discounts, not any provider's actual program terms; and nothing here is pricing or legal advice — it is a working sample of how the decisions get sequenced.
