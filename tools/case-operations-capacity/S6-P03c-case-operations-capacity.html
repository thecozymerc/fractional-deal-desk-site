Case Operations & Capacity.

A case queue fails quietly: cases age past their SLA targets, escalations pile up in one region, and the backlog number everyone quotes stops matching the register it came from. This tool is the control sheet for that queue — a 30-case register, a dashboard that recounts it live, and a capacity simulator that shows what intake and staffing do to the backlog next. Every case, date and figure in it is sample data.

Directions: Add or edit cases in the Case Register tab and read age, BREACH flags, totals, the corrected backlog, and regional breakdowns on the Backlog & SLA Dashboard, which recalculates from the register. Open cases age against today's date; closed cases measure Opened to Closed. On the Capacity Simulator tab, set intake, analyst count, throughput, complex-case share, and the complexity multiplier; read effective capacity, net clearance, days to clear, and the 30-day projection. The simulator's current backlog is linked live from the dashboard, so the projection starts from the queue as the register currently stands.

Inputs, logic, limits

Inputs: the register's columns — Region, Queue, Case Type, Priority, Status, Opened and Closed dates, and an SLA Target in days — plus the simulator's six inputs. Logic: the Age column and the SLA Flag (BREACH when age exceeds the target) are formulas; the dashboard counts statuses, computes the backlog as every case not Closed, counts BREACH flags, averages age, and breaks backlog, escalations, and breaches down by region. The simulator's logic, in its own words: effective capacity = analysts × cases/day, divided by the blended handling factor ((1 − complex share) + complex share × multiplier); net cleared = capacity − intake; days to clear = backlog ÷ net, rounded up; the projection subtracts net per day and floors at zero. Its limits, also its own: a simple linear planning model — it assumes intake and capacity stay constant and treats complexity only through the share × multiplier assumption, both of which the visitor sets. It plans staffing; it does not measure the live queue. One correction is carried in this website-build copy, and the dashboard names it in its own note: the Backlog KPI counts every case not Closed — 18 of the 30 sample cases — matching the by-region table.

Why a queue's numbers have to reconcile.

The questions a queue owner actually gets asked — how big is the backlog really, where is it breaching, which region is carrying the escalations — cannot be answered by scrolling a case list. They are answered by counts that reconcile: the backlog on the dashboard has to equal the by-region table underneath it, and both have to equal a hand count of the register. That reconciliation is not a nicety. An earlier version of this workbook's backlog formula counted a narrower set than its own region table did, and the two disagreed — the website-build copy corrects the KPI to count every case not Closed, and the dashboard carries a note saying so, in the open, where the number is quoted. A backlog figure that two parts of the same workbook compute differently is worse than no figure, because it gets quoted with confidence.

The simulator answers the second question — what happens next. Intake and capacity are the only two levers a queue owner really has, and the model's job is to make their trade-off visible before it is lived: at the sample settings the queue clears a few more cases a day than it takes in and the backlog drains in days; push intake past effective capacity and the same sheet says, in words, that the backlog grows at these settings and there is no clear date. The model is deliberately simple — constant intake, constant capacity, complexity as one blended factor — and it says so on its own tab, because a planning model that hides its assumptions produces confident staffing decisions on arithmetic nobody checked.
