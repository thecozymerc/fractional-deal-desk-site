Case Operations & Capacity

A case queue fails quietly: cases age past their SLA targets, escalations pile up in one region, and the backlog number everyone quotes stops matching the register it came from. This tool is the control sheet for that queue — a 30-case register, a dashboard that recounts it live, and a capacity simulator that shows what intake and staffing do to the backlog next. Every case, date and figure in it is sample data.

Provenance & Operating Record: Built from 15+ years in commercial contracts, deal desk, and revenue operations across contracts up to $300M and 3,000+ SKUs under management. This workbook is an equivalent sample built in October 2026 to pair with the portfolio document Case Operations Snapshot — Backlog & SLA Risk — the original workbook was not in Drive. The Capacity Simulator is a planning model built for this website; nothing in it is measured data. Open cases age against the visitor's current date (TODAY()), so breach counts and average age move with the calendar just as the spreadsheet's own formulas behave. All cases, dates, and figures below are fictional sample data.

The Queue Brief

Copyable plain-text queue summary: counts, regional breakdown, and capacity verdict


        


        

          
Inputs, logic, limits

          
Register & Dashboard: Inputs are the register's columns — Region, Queue, Case Type, Priority, Status, Opened and Closed dates, and an SLA Target in days. Age and SLA Flag (BREACH when age exceeds the target) are formulas; the dashboard counts statuses, computes the backlog as every case not Closed, counts BREACH flags, averages age, and breaks backlog, escalations, and breaches down by region.

          
Capacity Simulator: Inputs: current backlog (linked live from this workbook's dashboard), daily intake, analyst count, cases per analyst per day, complex-case share, and a complex-case handling multiplier. Logic: effective capacity = analysts × cases/day, divided by the blended handling factor ((1 − complex share) + complex share × multiplier); net cleared = capacity − intake; days to clear = backlog / net, rounded up; the projection subtracts net per day and floors at zero. Limits: a simple linear planning model — it assumes intake and capacity stay constant and treats complexity only through the share × multiplier assumption, both of which the visitor sets. It plans staffing; it does not measure the live queue.

Why a queue's numbers have to reconcile

        
Control mechanics for case backlogs and capacity planning

      


      

        

          
Counts that reconcile across every view

          
The questions a queue owner actually gets asked — how big is the backlog really, where is it breaching, which region carries the escalations — cannot be answered by scrolling a case list. They are answered by counts that reconcile: the backlog on the dashboard equals the sum of the by-region table underneath it, and both equal a hand count of the register. That reconciliation is not a nicety. An earlier version of this workbook's backlog formula counted a narrower set than its own region table did, and the two disagreed; a figure two parts of the same workbook compute differently is worse than no figure, because it gets quoted with confidence.

        

        

          
What happens next when intake meets capacity

          
The simulator answers what happens next. Intake and capacity are the only two levers a queue owner has, and the model makes their trade-off visible before it is lived: at the sample settings the queue clears a few more cases a day than it takes in and the backlog drains in days; push intake past effective capacity and the same sheet says, in words, that the backlog grows and there is no clear date. The model is deliberately simple — constant intake, constant capacity, complexity as one blended factor — and says so on its face, because a planning model that hides its assumptions produces confident staffing decisions on arithmetic nobody checked.
