The Mess — The deal is not stuck for the reason people say it is.

When a complex deal reaches the finish line and stalls, everyone usually has an explanation. Underneath, the basics are undone.

Paperwork is missing. Information is incomplete. Accounts are unverified. Sometimes the missing piece is the approval itself. Sometimes the check finds the opposite problem: a deal was approved that was not supposed to be.

The runbook exists, but it is too wordy to use in the moment. The process exists, but nobody is following the same version of it. Responsibilities are unclear, so the deal bounces between Deal Desk, Finance, Legal, and Fulfillment until it rots instead of booking.

That is the mess I fix.

How the mess gets made

Sales teams bring deals across the fence without doing the baseline work and expect Deal Desk to become their administrative buffer.

Early on, my mistake was trying to accommodate that. I chased the missing fields and unverified information myself. All that taught anyone was that Deal Desk would clean it up.

When I clamped down and held people to the submission standard, they escalated and tried to pull managers into the room to get me to look the other way. That is still better than the alternative.

If nobody holds the line, the deal drops into an endless loop. It gets pushed back, passed around, and slowly dies. That is deal paralysis.

Clean intake is not bureaucracy. It is how the deal gets done right the first time.

How I Work — The big work is reading the process.

I do not start by blaming the team that touched the deal last. I read the whole process and find where it actually broke.

I ask what we are doing and why. The why drives the action.

I check what the client has now, what the current deal is, whether this is new or a renewal, and whether they are eligible.

I look for the signal: what do the stakeholders actually want?

I check whether the stakeholders did their part. The tell is usually simple stuff: missing docs, missing info.

I find what is blocking the deal. Is something missing, or is a policy in the way?

I run the client’s own checklist in full. If they do not have one, that is the first finding.

If the blocker is policy, the question is practical: can we bend it, or do we need to break it and recreate it in a form that works?

Then I build the fix into the operation — ownership, decision rights, escalation, and consequences — so the same problem does not need a hero the next time.

The deal with no way forward

One enterprise deal reached me after it had already been escalated. There was no way forward. The standard rule required a specific domain format, but the customer needed the service to work with its existing domains so it could test the service with its products.

I kept escalating, pulled the right stakeholders in, and pushed new steps through until the workaround was approved and a team was assigned to build it. The customer got its test running. The approved workaround then became the runbook for later complex accounts.

That is the pattern: find the real blocker, push through the right door, and leave a process behind instead of a one-time exception.
